# Federal Title IX / VI Baseline

**As of:** 2026-04-01
**Sources:** Public Counsel, *Navigating the Title IX Process: A Guide for Student
Survivors of Sexual Violence in College/University Settings* (published June 2026,
current as of April 1, 2026); U.S. Department of Education, *Title IX Final Rule Overview*.
**Scope:** General federal framework. Each school runs its own process with its own
rules and timelines — always confirm specifics with the school's Title IX office.

> This is general information, not legal advice. Laws and regulations change. For advice
> about a specific situation, talk to an attorney or a survivor advocate.

## What Title IX is
Title IX is a federal law prohibiting sex-based discrimination at schools that receive
federal funding. Sex-based discrimination includes sexual harassment, sexual violence,
and denial of reasonable accommodations for pregnant/lactating students. Most schools
have a Title IX office that receives and investigates reports; these proceedings are often
called "Title IX proceedings."

Note: some recent federal court rulings have limited Title IX's scope regarding gender
identity (see *Tennessee v. Cardona*, 2025). State laws (e.g., California) may provide
additional protections based on gender identity and sexual orientation.

## What counts as sexual harassment (federal definition)
- **Quid pro quo:** a school employee conditioning an educational benefit/service on
  participation in unwelcome sexual conduct.
- **Hostile environment:** unwelcome conduct a reasonable person would find so severe,
  pervasive, and objectively offensive that it denies equal educational access.
- **Sexual assault, dating violence, domestic violence, or stalking** (as defined in the
  Clery Act and the Violence Against Women Act).

## Supportive measures (available with or without a formal complaint)
Schools must offer free, individualized supportive measures to every complainant, even
if they never file a formal complaint. Examples: academic extensions, excused absences,
class/schedule changes, housing changes, leave of absence, campus escort, and **no-contact
orders**. A no-contact order is a school administrative measure (one-way or mutual) — it is
**different from a court restraining order**, which only a court can issue.

## Key people
- **Complainant** — person filing/affected. **Respondent** — person complained about.
- **Title IX Coordinator** — ensures the school complies with Title IX.
- **Investigator** — investigates the complaint. **Hearing Officer** — runs the hearing and
  decides. **Appellate Officer** — reviews appeals.
- **Advisor** — supports you at every stage and (at many schools) conducts
  cross-examination at the hearing; may be an attorney or a non-attorney (friend, mentor).
  If a school allows advisor-conducted questioning, it must provide an advisor if you don't
  have one. **Support person** — provides emotional support; does not speak for you.

## The process (common shape; schools vary)
1. **Reporting** — via web form, email, or phone to the Title IX office. Reporting does NOT
   require filing a formal complaint. The formal written document is the "complaint."
2. **Initial interview** — the office gathers details, offers resources, and discusses
   options. You can take time to decide; you can request accommodations even without a
   formal complaint.
3. **Notice to respondent** — if you file, the respondent gets written notice (summary,
   policy at issue, rights, process) and is **presumed not responsible** pending the outcome
   (this is a procedural presumption, not a prejudgment against you).
4. **Investigation** — "reasonably prompt timeframe" (no fixed federal definition — ask for
   an estimate). Interviews of complainant, respondent, witnesses; you may submit evidence
   and identify witnesses.
5. **Evidence review** — you receive a summary of the evidence and can object to how it's
   described or to inclusions/exclusions.
6. **Investigation report** — final report; may or may not include a preliminary
   determination, which you can challenge.
7. **Hearing** (postsecondary) — live hearing with advisor cross-examination (parties never
   question each other directly). Some schools allow remote/Zoom participation to limit
   exposure.
8. **Decision/outcome** — written determination. If responsibility is found, sanctions may
   include reprimand, housing/class changes, no-contact order, probation, suspension,
   withholding of degree, or expulsion.
9. **Appeal** — both parties may appeal; watch the deadline. You generally must exhaust the
   school's internal appeal before suing the school over how it handled the matter.

## Due-process features the federal rule requires of the grievance process
Written notice of allegations; equal chance to pick an advisor and to submit/review
evidence; trained, unbiased personnel; privacy protection for treatment records (written
consent required); voluntary written consent for any informal resolution (and none where an
employee is alleged to have harassed a student); presumption of non-responsibility; a
stated evidentiary standard (preponderance *or* clear-and-convincing, applied consistently);
separation of investigator and decision-maker (no single-investigator model); "rape shield"
protections on prior sexual history; written determination; remedies if responsible; equal
right to appeal; protection from retaliation; recordkeeping.

## Your rights (non-exhaustive)
- Access and understand the school's policies.
- A timely, comprehensive investigation.
- Choose your resolution path — the school should not pressure you toward informal
  resolution/mediation.
- Talk freely about your experience (no gag orders).
- Be informed of the outcome.
- Review and respond to evidence.
- Appeal sanctions and the decision.
- Have an advisor (and possibly a support person) at meetings and the hearing.
- Be free from retaliation by the respondent or school staff.

If you think a right was violated, document it (dates, notes, evidence) — useful for an
appeal or lawsuit. You can advocate for yourself by citing the school's own policy or
federal policy to the office.

## Informal resolution
Voluntary mediation/restorative process requiring both parties' written consent. A school
cannot require it, and it is not available where an employee allegedly harassed a student.
Some states bar it for sexual-violence cases (e.g., California Education Code § 66262.5).

## Deadlines & lawsuits
There is **no statute of limitations on filing a Title IX complaint with your school** — but
the school's power to sanction shrinks once a respondent graduates or leaves, so some
schools dismiss complaints then. Separately, **civil lawsuits** (against a respondent or the
school) have their own statutes of limitations that vary by claim and state — consult an
attorney. Title IX is an administrative process; a civil suit can seek financial compensation.

## Finding the Title IX office
Every covered school must designate a Title IX Coordinator and publish their contact info
and the non-discrimination policy. Search "[school name] Title IX office" or check the
school's website. You may also file externally with the U.S. Department of Education Office
for Civil Rights (OCR).
